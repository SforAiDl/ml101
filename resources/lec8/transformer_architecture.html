The Transformer Architecture (and its Elements)

Manit Tanwar

Recall

Three things from last lecture
  • Attention: every token makes a query, a key and a value. Weights = softmax of query·key, scaled. Output = weighted sum of values.
  • Masked self-attention: add $-\infty$ to scores above the diagonal. Token $i$ can't look at tokens after $i$.
  • Cross-attention: queries come from one sequence (decoder), keys and values from another (encoder).
$$\text{Attn}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

Part 1Multi-head attention

  • With a single attention head, the model is only able to learn a single 'view' of the data.
  • Is there some way to add more expressivity?

The mechanism

  • Every head reads the full $X$.
  • Each head has its own $W_q, W_k, W_v$. They project $X$ down to $d/h$ dimensions.
  • Each head runs ordinary attention in its own small space.
  • Concatenate the outputs, then mix them with one more matrix, $W^O$.

$n=4$ tokens, $d=8$, $h=4$ heads, so each head works in $d/h=2$ dimensions. Real models: $d=512$, $h=8$, so 64 per head.

Is it more expensive?
  • No. $h$ heads of size $d/h$ cost about the same as one head of size $d$.
  • Heads are cheaper individually (smaller dot products), and they run in parallel.
  • $W^O$ allows mixing of information across heads.

Do trained heads really split the work?

Specifically chosen heads with real attention weights. Other heads are messier.

Does MHA actually help?

Source: Vaswani et al. 2017, Table 3 row (A).

  • More isn't always better. Quality plateaus at 8 to 16, then drops at 32 (heads become too small to be useful).
Limitations
  • Michel et al. (2019), Are Sixteen Heads Really Better Than One?, found that at test time you can delete most heads from a trained model with little loss.
  • So heads help during training. They aren't all individually important afterwards.
  • This is also why GQA and MQA (Part 3b) get away with sharing keys and values across heads.

Part 2Positional encoding

Attention can't tell order

  • Without positional information, shuffling the sequence produces the same set of vectors, just reordered.
  • Attention itself is a function on sets. Order must be injected externally.

Absolute encodings

The original method: build a vector $PE_{pos}$ for every position, and add it to the token embedding before the first layer.

  • Each position needs a unique signature.
  • Values must stay bounded, so they don't dominate the embeddings.
  • Nearby positions should look similar.

Intuition: binary counters

$$\begin{aligned}PE_{(pos,\,2i)}&=\sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)\\[6pt]PE_{(pos,\,2i+1)}&=\cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)\end{aligned}$$
  • There are no learned parameters involved here, just a fixed table.
  • An alternative would be to learn a table. The authors tried both, and found no meaningful difference.

What's wrong with absolute?

  • Positions are tied to the table. Train on 512 tokens, then ask for token 2000, and you're out of distribution. A learned table has no row 2000 at all. Sinusoids exist at 2000, but the model never saw those values.
  • Added to the input once. The signal gets mixed with content and has to survive every layer.
  • Absolute, not relative. What we usually care about is "the word 3 back", not "the word at position 47".

Relative encodings: RoPE

  • Idea: make the score depend on $m-n$ by construction.
  • Don't add anything to the embeddings. Instead, rotate q and k by an angle proportional to their position.
  • Dot product of two vectors depends only on the angle between them. Rotate q by $m\theta$, k by $n\theta$, and the angle between them is $(m-n)\theta$.
$$\langle R_{m\theta}\,q,\;R_{n\theta}\,k\rangle=\langle q,\;R_{(n-m)\theta}\,k\rangle$$
  • Split q into $d_k/2$ pairs. Each pair rotates at its own speed $\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d_k}$.
  • Applied to q and k only. Not v. Position affects where to look, not what to read.
  • Applied inside every attention layer, after the head split. Not just once before the first layer.
  • Again, no parameters to be learned.

How this fixes the problem

ProblemWith RoPE
Score depends on $m$ and $n$ separatelyDepends only on $m-n$. Exactly, not approximately.
Signal added once, mixed with contentNothing added. Position is applied to q and k at every layer.
Unseen absolute positions at test timeModel only ever sees offsets. Helps, but doesn't fully solve it (see below).
Limitations of RoPE

RoPE alone still degrades past the trained length. Large offsets give rotation angles it has never seen.

Other relative schemes
  • Shaw et al. (2018): learn a vector per offset $m-n$, add it inside attention.
  • T5 bias: learn one scalar per offset (bucketed), add it to the score.
  • ALiBi: no learning. Subtract $\lambda\cdot|m-n|$ from the score. Far tokens get penalised linearly.

Part 3The architecture

Redrawn from Figure 1 of Vaswani et al. 2017. The next sections zoom into each piece.

Residual connections

  • Plain stack: $y=f(x)$. Gradient has to pass through every layer's Jacobian. Multiply enough numbers smaller than 1 and it vanishes.
  • Residual: $y=x+f(x)$. Then $\partial y/\partial x = I + \partial f/\partial x$. The identity term is a highway that always gets through.

Add & Norm

  • Add: the residual from above. Norm: LayerNorm, to keep activations at a stable scale.
  • The question is where the norm goes.
$$\begin{aligned}\text{Post-norm (paper):}\quad &x_{l+1}=\text{LN}\big(x_l+F(x_l)\big)\\[6pt]\text{Pre-norm (today):}\quad &x_{l+1}=x_l+F\big(\text{LN}(x_l)\big)\end{aligned}$$
Show:
  • Xiong et al. (2020): in post-norm, gradients at the last layers are huge compared to early layers at init. That's why the original needed a careful learning-rate warmup. Pre-norm gradients are well-behaved, so warmup mostly goes away and big learning rates are fine.
  • Today almost everyone uses pre-norm (BERT was post-norm; OPT-350M is a rare holdout).
Note
  • Some recent models add a second norm after the sublayer, but still inside the branch, off the residual stream (Gemma 2, Grok; OLMo 2 only does this one).
  • Rule: keep the residual stream clean.

LayerNorm v/s RMSNorm

  • LayerNorm: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation, then scale and shift.
  • RMSNorm: only divide by the root mean square. No mean, no bias.
  • Fewer operations, less memory traffic, and in practice just as good. Used by LLaMA, PaLM, T5.
$$\begin{aligned}\text{LN}(x)&=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\odot\gamma+\beta\\[8pt]\text{RMSNorm}(x)&=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac1d\sum_i x_i^2+\epsilon}}\odot\gamma\end{aligned}$$

Feedforward

  • Attention is responsible for determining 'how much' tokens attend to each other.
  • The FFN 'processes' each token on its own.
$$\begin{aligned}\text{FFN}(x)&=\text{ReLU}(xW_1+b_1)\,W_2+b_2\\[6pt]W_1&\in\mathbb R^{d\times 4d},\quad W_2\in\mathbb R^{4d\times d}\end{aligned}$$

Why do we need it?

  • Nonlinearity. Attention itself is a linear transformation. The ReLU in the FFN introduces the required nonlinearity.
  • Expressivity and Capacity. The FFN projects to a higher dimension then back. Intuition: higher dimension with more params allows greater expressibility.

What does N× mean?

  • The grey box is one block. "N×" means stack $N$ of them.
  • In series, not in parallel. Block 1's output is block 2's input.
  • Each block has its own weights. They are not shared copies.
  • In the decoder, every block's cross-attention reads the final encoder output.
Layers $N$:6

N=6 in the paper. BERT-base 12. LLaMA-2 70B has 80. GPT-3 has 96.

Wrap-upPutting it together

  • Multi-head attention: several smaller attentions in parallel, so each token can look in several places at once. Then mix with $W^O$.
  • Positional encoding: attention is order-blind. Sinusoids add position to the input; RoPE rotates q and k so scores depend on the offset.
  • Architecture: a residual stream, with attention and FFN sublayers reading from it and adding back. Repeat $N$ times, in series.
  • Since 2017: pre-norm, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, no biases, GQA, decoder-only.
