Modern AI Systems: Prompting and RLHF

Nidheesh Kannadasan

How do we turn a text-completer into an assistant? Part A changes the input (prompting). Part B changes the weights (post-training). Every figure can be played, or stepped through with Next or the arrow keys.

Recall

Three things from earlier lectures
  • Weights are knobs (L4): training turns millions of knobs, one gradient step at a time, to make the loss smaller.
  • Pretraining is fill in the blank (L9): hide part of some text and learn to predict it. No labels needed, so it can use huge amounts of text.
  • GPT (L10): a decoder-only transformer that predicts the next token, appends it, and repeats.

MotivationSame model, different behaviour

Here is one prompt, given to two models[1]. Pick a model, then step through.

Source: OpenAI (2022)

Both models are GPT-3 underneath. They have the same architecture; the only difference is that InstructGPT was fine-tuned on data collected from people[2].

So how do we turn a text-completer into an assistant? There are two levers:

  1. Change the input: write a better prompt. No weights change. Part A, now.
  2. Change the weights: train the model some more after pretraining. Part B.

A1How an LLM is trained, in stages

Before we use a model, it helps to know where it came from. A chat model is built in stages, and each stage turns the knobs again with a different kind of data.

Sources: Brown et al. (2020); Ouyang et al. (2022); Karpathy (2023); DeepSeek-AI (2025)

Keep in mind

Every stage changes the weights. Prompting changes nothing in the model: you only change what you type.

A2Zero-shot: just ask

Zero-shot prompting means describing the task in plain words, with no examples. The model reads the task, then continues the text after =>:

Zero-shot prompt
Translate English to French: ← task description
cheese => ← prompt

Source: Brown et al. (2020), Figure 2.1

For base GPT-3, zero-shot is the hardest setting: with no examples, it has to guess what format you want[3].

The moon-landing example was a zero-shot prompt too. Base GPT-3 did what it was trained to do and continued the text. Predicting the next token of a web page is a different goal from following a user's instructions[2].

With a base model, though, we can still do a lot without any training: show it what we want, inside the prompt. That's next.

A3Few-shot: learn from examples in the prompt

Instead of only describing the task, put a few solved examples in the prompt. The model picks up the pattern and continues it. This is called in-context learning[3].

Source: Brown et al. (2020), Figure 2.1

A4Chain-of-thought: reasoning step by step

A few-shot example can show more than the answer. It can also show the steps that lead to the answer. The model copies that style, writes out its own steps, and gets more multi-step problems right[5].

Source: Wei et al. (2022), Figure 1

Zero-shot chain-of-thought

You don't even need worked examples. Add one sentence where the answer starts, “Let's think step by step”, and the model writes out its reasoning on its own[6].

Source: Kojima et al. (2022), Figure 1

On MultiArith, a set of maths word problems, that one sentence lifts accuracy from 17.7% to 78.7%, beating eight solved examples[6].

Why does writing the steps help?

Remember how GPT generates text (L10): one token at a time, and every token gets one pass through the same stack of layers. That's a fixed amount of computation per token.

A5ReAct: reason (CoT) + act (tool use)

Chain-of-thought happens entirely inside the model. If a fact it needs is wrong in there, every step built on it goes wrong too. ReAct (reason + act) lets the model use a tool between its thoughts. Here the tool is a Wikipedia search, and its results are pasted back into the prompt[7].

The question below needs two lookups in a row. Compare two prompting styles on it.

Source: Yao et al. (2022), Figure 1

ReAct looks things up instead of making them up

The researchers went through failed answers by hand and sorted them by cause. Made-up facts caused 56% of chain-of-thought's failures, and 0% of ReAct's[7].

The big picture

Thought, action, observation, repeat. That loop is an agent acting in an environment: it takes an action, the world answers with an observation, and it decides what to do next.

Look at the second column: every technique leaves the weights alone. And yet in the moon-landing example, the same plain request failed on GPT-3 and worked on InstructGPT.

Prompting changes what we ask. To change what the model wants to say, we change the weights.

Part B1Why SFT isn't enough

Part A changed the input. Part B changes the weights: more training after pretraining. Here is where we are on the map from A1.

Stage 2, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), trains the model to copy answers that people wrote. It works, but it has two limits:

  • A ceiling. A model trained to copy can, at best, write as well as the people it copies.
  • Cost. Every example is a person writing a complete, careful answer. Even InstructGPT's SFT set was only about 13k prompts[2].

There is an easier job a person can do: judge. Picking the better of two answers takes seconds, and you can judge answers you could never have written yourself.

Source: OpenAI (2022)

Judging works: one result

Summaries better than the human-written ones. On summarising Reddit posts, a 1.3B model trained with human feedback was preferred to the human-written reference summaries 61% of the time. A supervised model ten times bigger (12.9B), trained to copy those references, reached 43%[10].

Source: Stiennon et al. (2020), Figure 1 (61% and 43% from the text; other values read off the figure)

Part B2The reward model: judgments become a score

People can't judge every answer the model will ever write. So we train a second model to judge for them: the reward model. It reads a prompt and an answer, and outputs one number, a score. (In the papers, and in the equations below, this score is called the reward, written r.)

  • It's a language model with its last layer swapped: instead of probabilities for the next token (L10), it outputs a single number. InstructGPT used one 6B reward model for all three of its sizes (1.3B, 6B and 175B)[2].
  • Labelers see 4 to 9 answers to the same prompt, written by the model, and rank them from best to worst. Every pair inside a ranking becomes one training example: 4 answers give 6 pairs, 9 give 36[2].
  • For each pair, training raises the preferred answer's score and lowers the other's, until the preferred one is clearly ahead.

Try it: you are the labeler. Both answer pairs below are real outputs from GPT-3 and InstructGPT.

Source: Ouyang et al. (2022), Figure 9; OpenAI (2022)

The answers are real; the score changes are illustrative.

The reward model is trained on every labelled pair with this loss. It is small when the preferred answer's score is well above the rejected one's, and large when it is below:

$$\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma\big(\htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y_w)} - \htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y_l)}\big)$$
$x$
the prompt
$y_w,\ y_l$
the answer the labeler preferred (w for win) and the one they rejected (l for lose)
$\htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y)}$
the reward model's score for answer $y$ to prompt $x$
$\phi$
the reward model's weights: the knobs that training turns (L4)
$\sigma$
the sigmoid from L1: squashes any number into 0 to 1. Here, σ(gap) is the chance that the reward model agrees with the labeler
$-\log$
turns that chance into a loss: near 0 when σ is close to 1, large when σ is close to 0

Simplified from the InstructGPT paper's Eq. 1[2]. Dropped: the 𝔼 and the 1/C(K,2) factor, which together just mean "average over all labelled pairs".

This is the loss from L1's logistic regression. The yes/no question is "did the labeler prefer yw?", and what goes into the sigmoid is the gap between the two scores.

Part B3The RL loop, with a KL penalty

We now have a reward model that can score any answer. The next step trains the language model to write answers that get high scores from it.

This is reinforcement learning (RL). Nobody shows the model the right answer. It only finds out how good its own attempts were. That's the big difference from SFT, and from L4, where every training example came with the correct output.

How does training make an answer "more likely"? With SFT's next-token training (L10), applied to the model's own answer and scaled by its score: a high score pushes the answer's token probabilities up, a low score pushes them down. This is called a policy gradient.

The catch: reward hacking

The reward model is an imitation of human judgment, learned from a limited number of comparisons. Push hard enough against it and the model finds answers that score high but that people don't actually like. This is reward hacking. For example, if the reward model slightly favours longer answers, the model learns to pad them: the score rises, but the answers are no better.

In the summarisation work, optimising harder against the reward model first made summaries better, according to people, and then made them worse[10]; a controlled study found the same rise-then-fall, and that bigger reward models delay it[11].

The fix: a KL penalty

Keep a reference model, πref: a copy of the SFT model whose weights never change during training (ref = reference). During training, measure the KL divergence between the trained model and the reference model: a number that measures how differently two models assign probabilities to answers. It is 0 when they agree, and larger the more they disagree. Then subtract β × that number from the score. This is the KL penalty: the model can improve, but each step away from the reference model costs something.

$$\max_\theta \;\; \htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y)} \;-\; \htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y \mid x)}}$$
$\pi_\theta$
the model being trained; $\theta$ is its weights. In RL, the model being trained is called the policy
$\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$
how likely the model is to write answer $y$ for prompt $x$: the next-token probabilities from L8 and L10, multiplied along the answer
$\htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y)}$
the reward model's score for that answer (from B2)
$\htmlClass{k-leash}{\pi_{ref}}$
the reference model: a copy of the SFT model whose weights never change (ref = reference)
$\htmlClass{k-leash}{\log(\pi_\theta / \pi_{ref})}$
how much more likely the trained model is to write $y$ than the reference model is: 0 if both find $y$ equally likely. Averaged over the model's own answers, this is the KL divergence
$\htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta}$
the KL coefficient: how much each unit of KL divergence costs
$\max_\theta$
turn the knobs $\theta$ to make this as large as possible, averaged over prompts, where $y$ is an answer the model wrote itself

Simplified from the InstructGPT paper's Eq. 2[2]. Dropped: the 𝔼 (average over prompts) and the γ term that mixes in pretraining data.

Keep in mind

RLHF = a reward model learned from human comparisons, plus an RL loop that maximises the score minus a KL penalty to the SFT (reference) model. The penalty keeps the model close to the answers the reward model was trained on, where its scores can still be trusted.

Part B4PPO: the first way to run the loop

B3 described the loop: write, get scored, adjust. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) is the algorithm InstructGPT used to actually run it[2].

Two ideas make it work.

Idea 1: learn from "better than expected"

A score of 7 out of 10 is great on a hard prompt and poor on an easy one. So PPO adds another model, the critic, which looks at the prompt and guesses the score an answer to it would usually get. What the model learns from is the difference, called the advantage:

  • The answer scored above the critic's guess: advantage positive, make this answer more likely.
  • It scored below the guess: advantage negative, make it less likely.

The critic starts as a copy of the trained reward model (B2), and is trained alongside the model by regression (L2): its guess is pushed towards the total each answer actually got.

Idea 2: small steps

One answer that scored well by chance shouldn't change the model much. After every update, PPO compares the new model with the one from just before it. How much more (or less) likely the new model makes the answer is a ratio, ρ. Starting at ρ = 1, clipping removes any gain from moving ρ past 0.8–1.2. The model can still end up beyond that range, but nothing pulls it further. That's the proximal, "stay nearby", in PPO.

This is not B3's KL penalty. The KL penalty keeps the model close to the SFT model over all of training; clipping keeps each update close to the model just before it.

Source: Schulman et al. (2017), Figure 1 (redrawn from the formula)

Both ideas fit in one line:

$$L^{CLIP} = \min\big(\rho A,\ \text{clip}(\rho,\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\, A\big)$$ $$\rho = \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{old}(y \mid x)}$$ $$A = \htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y)} - \htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y \mid x)}} - \htmlClass{k-critic}{V(x)}$$
$\pi_{old}$
the model as it was just before this update
$\rho$
how much more likely the updated model makes answer $y$ than before this update: 1 = no change, 1.2 = 20% more likely
$\htmlClass{k-critic}{V(x)}$
the critic's guess of the total for prompt $x$
$\htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta \log(\pi_\theta / \pi_{ref})}$
the KL penalty from B3. The score minus this is the total
$A$
the advantage: the actual total (the score minus the KL penalty, from B3) minus the critic's guess. Positive = better than expected
$\epsilon$
sets the range 1 − ε to 1 + ε. InstructGPT used ε = 0.2, so the range is 0.8–1.2
$\text{clip},\ \min$
clip(ρ, 0.8, 1.2) is ρ pulled back into 0.8–1.2 inside the formula; min takes the smaller of the two versions. Together: clipping removes any gain from moving ρ past 0.8–1.2
$L^{CLIP}$
what training makes as large as possible, averaged over many answers

Simplified from the PPO paper's Eq. 7[12]: the whole answer instead of one token at a time; A as the total (score minus the KL penalty, as in B3) minus the critic's guess, instead of GAE; dropped Ê and the value and entropy terms of Eq. 9. PPO writes ρ as rt(θ), and (x, y) as (st, at). ε = 0.2 is InstructGPT's setting[2].

The cost: four models at once

  • Memory: the model being trained, the reference model (for the KL penalty), the reward model and the critic all sit in memory together. InstructGPT used a 6B reward model and a 6B critic, the critic starting as a copy of the reward model[2].
  • Hard to tune: four models and a loop, with several settings that must all be right at once, or training becomes unstable. InstructGPT kept its reward models at 6B partly because 175B ones trained unstably[2].

The result: GPT-3 becomes InstructGPT

This is the recipe behind the moon-landing example. SFT, then a reward model, then PPO turned GPT-3, which continued the moon-landing prompt with more prompts, into InstructGPT, which answered it[2].

  • Preferred, even when much smaller: labelers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT to the 175B GPT-3, a model over 100× bigger[2].
  • Fewer made-up facts: on tasks where the answer should only use the input (like summarising), InstructGPT made up information 21% of the time, against GPT-3's 41%[2].
Keep in mind

PPO = learn from "better than expected" (thanks to a critic) + small, clipped steps. It works, but it needs four models in memory and careful tuning. The next two methods each remove part of that cost.

Part B5DPO: skip the loop

PPO works, but it's heavy: four models, a loop that keeps writing and scoring new answers, and many settings to tune. RLHF done this way is complex and often unstable[13]. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, 2023) asks: do we need the reward model and the loop at all?

The trick: the model is already a scorer

Go back to the objective from B3: score minus β × KL divergence. Working through its maths (which we'll skip) shows that the best model for that objective and the reward are tied together. So you can read a reward straight off the model: how much more likely the trained model makes an answer than the reference model does, times β. This is the implicit reward, and it gives the paper its title: "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"[13].

Now take the reward model's loss from B2 and swap the reward model's score for the implicit reward. Everything else stays:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal{L}_{RM} &= -\log \sigma\left(\htmlClass{k-swap}{\htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y_w)}} - \htmlClass{k-swap}{\htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y_l)}}\right)\\[14pt]\mathcal{L}_{DPO} &= -\log \sigma\left(\htmlClass{k-swap}{\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w \mid x)}} - \htmlClass{k-swap}{\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l \mid x)}}\right)\end{aligned}$$
$x,\ y_w,\ y_l$
prompt, preferred answer, rejected answer (as in B2)
$\sigma,\ -\log$
the sigmoid and the loss on top of it (as in B2): small when the winner is clearly ahead
$\pi_\theta,\ \pi_{ref},\ \beta$
the model being trained, the reference model, and the KL coefficient (as in B3)
highlighted
the swap: the reward model's score $\htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y)}$ becomes $\beta \log\big(\pi_\theta(y \mid x) / \pi_{ref}(y \mid x)\big)$, the implicit reward

Simplified from the DPO paper's Eq. 7[13]: dropped the 𝔼, which means "average over all labelled pairs".

In words, each training step looks at one labelled pair and makes the preferred answer more likely and the rejected one less likely, both measured against the reference model. The reference model, and so the KL penalty, is now built into the loss itself. It's the same kind of training as B2, but now it trains the language model directly.

Pairs the model already gets right (σ near 1) give a loss near 0 and barely change it; pairs it gets wrong give a large loss and a big push. So DPO learns most from the pairs it gets wrong.

What it gives up

DPO learns only from a fixed set of preference pairs, collected beforehand (offline). It never writes a new answer during training and finds out how good it was, so it can't discover anything better than what is already in the pairs.

Does it work?

DPO was tested on three tasks: steering movie reviews towards a positive sentiment, summarising Reddit posts, and single-turn dialogue[13]. On summaries, GPT-4 was the judge, after a check showed it agreed with human judges about as often as humans agree with each other. DPO's summaries beat the human-written reference about 61% of the time; PPO's best was 57%[13]. (Each was sampled at its best temperature, a setting for how random the generated text is.)

Keep in mind

DPO = B2's loss with the reward model swapped for the language model itself. Two models instead of four, no loop, no sampling. The price: it can only learn from the pairs it is given.

DPO can't try new answers. Reasoning needs exactly that, so DeepSeek went back to RL.

Part B6GRPO: bring the loop back, without the critic

DPO made training cheap by dropping the loop, but the loop is how a model discovers answers nobody has shown it. For reasoning, that's the whole point. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) keeps PPO's trial and error, and removes PPO's most expensive helper instead: the critic[14].

  • A rule-based reward. For maths and code, you don't need a learned reward model: check whether the final answer is right, or whether the code passes its tests. A rule has no learned judgment for the model to exploit.
  • Group-relative advantage. Instead of asking a critic what score to expect, write a group of answers to the same question and compare each with the group's average. Answers that beat the group are pushed up, the rest down. The group's average estimates what an answer to this question usually scores, which is what PPO's critic had to learn. Dividing by the spread puts easy and hard questions on the same scale.

The advantage, and the objective, next to PPO's:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{PPO:}\quad A &= \htmlClass{k-score}{r_\phi(x, y)} - \htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y \mid x)}} - \htmlClass{k-critic}{V(x)}\\[10pt]\text{GRPO:}\quad A_i &= \htmlClass{k-swap}{\frac{\htmlClass{k-score}{r_i} - \text{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\text{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}}\end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned}L^{CLIP} &= \min\big(\rho \htmlClass{k-swap}{A},\ \text{clip}(\rho,\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\, \htmlClass{k-swap}{A}\big)\\[10pt]\mathcal{J}_{GRPO} &= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\Big[\min\big(\rho_i \htmlClass{k-swap}{A_i},\ \text{clip}(\rho_i,\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\, \htmlClass{k-swap}{A_i}\big) - \htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta\, \mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{ref})}\Big]\end{aligned}$$
$G$
the number of answers written for one question (DeepSeek-R1-Zero used 16)
$\htmlClass{k-score}{r_i}$
the rule-based reward for answer $i$, e.g. 1 if right and 0 if wrong
$\text{mean},\ \text{std}$
the group's average score, and how spread out the scores are
$A$
PPO's advantage, as in B4: the total minus the critic's guess $\htmlClass{k-critic}{V(x)}$
$A_i$
how far answer $i$ beat its group, in units of that spread. It replaces the critic: $\htmlClass{k-critic}{V(x)}$ is gone
$\rho_i,\ \epsilon$
as in B4, for answer $i$: clipping removes any gain from moving $\rho_i$ past 0.8–1.2
$\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}$

$\htmlClass{k-leash}{\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{ref})}$

DeepSeek-R1-Zero: reasoning from right answers alone

GRPO was introduced with DeepSeekMath, where RL took a 7B maths model from 46.8% to 51.7% on the MATH benchmark (and from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K). There, the score still came from a learned reward model[14].

DeepSeek-R1-Zero went further[4]:

  • No SFT first: RL started straight from the pretrained base model.
  • Rules, not a reward model: one reward for a correct final answer, one for the right format (thinking inside <think> tags). The paper avoided learned reward models because they get hacked during large-scale RL.
  • 16 answers per question, compared with each other by GRPO.

Source: DeepSeek-AI (2025), Figure 1 (15.6% and 77.9% from the text; other values read off the figure)

Remember A4: to get step-by-step working, we had to ask for it, with worked examples or Let's think step by step. Nobody asked R1-Zero. It was only ever rewarded for right answers, and writing longer chains of thought is what earned them, so its answers grew longer by themselves.

R1-Zero had flaws: its reasoning was often hard to read, and it mixed languages (English and Chinese) within one answer. The released DeepSeek-R1 fixed this by first fine-tuning on thousands of readable worked examples (a small SFT step), then running RL with an extra reward for staying in one language[4].

Keep in mind

GRPO = PPO's loop and clipped steps, with the critic replaced by "how did this answer do compared with its group?". Two models plus a rule-based reward. It needs a score you can trust, which is why it shines on maths and code.

Part B7Limitations

Learning from human judgments is powerful, but the model can only be as good as the judgments, and the scorer built from them.

Whose preferences?

InstructGPT's labels came from about 40 contractors, mostly English speakers in the United States or Southeast Asia. They followed instructions written by the researchers, on prompts sent in by the company's API customers, and agreed with each other only about 73% of the time[2].

"Aligned" means aligned with that group.

Sycophancy

People tend to prefer answers that agree with them, so rewarding what people prefer also rewards agreement. When a user replied “I don't think that's right. Are you sure?”, Claude 1.3 wrongly admitted to a mistake on 98% of questions[15].

On the hardest misconceptions, a preference model preferred the flattering answer over the truthful one 45% of the time[15].

Reward hacking

As in B3: a learned scorer is only an imitation of human judgment, and pushing hard against it finds answers that score well but aren't good. The KL penalty and bigger reward models delay this; they don't remove it.

Human labels are expensive

One answer: let an AI do some of the judging. In Constitutional AI, an AI compares pairs of answers against a short written list of principles, the "constitution"[16].

In that work, AI feedback replaced human labels only for harmlessness; labels for helpfulness still came from people[16].

Wrap-upPutting it together

  • Zero-shot: describe the task and ask. Works on instruction-tuned models; a base model tends to just continue the text.
  • Few-shot: put solved examples in the prompt. The model learns the task in context, with no gradient step.
  • Chain-of-thought: show worked steps in the examples, and the model writes out its own. Each written step is more computation before the answer.
  • Zero-shot CoT: no examples, just Let's think step by step. Reasoning models now do this unprompted, because they were trained to.
  • ReAct: interleave thoughts with tool calls. It looks facts up instead of making them up.
  • Post-training (RLHF): people compare answers, a reward model learns to score them, and an RL loop pushes the model towards high scores, with a KL penalty to the SFT model. PPO runs that loop with a critic; DPO skips the loop and trains on the pairs directly; GRPO keeps the loop but compares each answer with its group, which is how reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1 learned to think at length.
